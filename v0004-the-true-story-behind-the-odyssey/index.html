Was Anything in The Odyssey Actually Real?

A three-thousand-year-old poem needs some paperwork. Here is each claim in the video, the source behind it, and the places where we simplified. Click a time to jump to that moment in the video.

Watch the video101 claims, 7 chaptersUpdated 2026-10-25
A note on simplifying. The Odyssey is a poem, so most of what we cite is the poem itself, and where the video says "sort of" this page says why. Where the evidence is a modern guess (the dwarf-elephant skull, the snowdrop), the video calls it a guess and so does this page. Every adventure at sea is told by Odysseus himself, which is why the video stamps them "according to Odysseus". Dates are rounded, and we say "about" where sources differ. Several sources are translations hosted by libraries, encyclopedia entries or university pages rather than new studies, and we say so when it matters. Found a mistake? Tell us in the video's comments and we will fix this page.

0:01Is Anything in the Odyssey Real?

0:01
The one-eyed giant? The witch who turns men into pigs?

Both are in the poem: the Cyclops Polyphemus in Book 9 and the enchantress Circe, who turns men into pigs, in Book 10. Homer only implies that the Cyclops has one eye (the blinding scene). Hesiod says it outright, but for his own Cyclopes, Zeus's smiths, a different group. "Witch" is our word: Circe is a goddess and an enchantress.

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.106-115 and 9.287-298 (the Cyclops) (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
  • Homer, Odyssey 10.233-240 (Circe) (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D10%3Acard%3D229
  • Hesiod, Theogony 139-145 (Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0130:card=139
0:06
It turns out, some of it is. Not the pigs, sadly.

"Some of it" means what the video later stamps REAL: the burned city at Troy, Cape Malea, the Strait of Messina, the palace at Pylos and the boar's-tusk helmet. Each has its own sources in its own chapter below. The pigs are the joke: the transformation is the poem's magic, so it is MYTH.

  • Homer, Odyssey 10.233-240 (Circe turns the men into pigs) (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D10%3Acard%3D229
0:12
In Greek tombs more than three thousand years old, archaeologists have found the pieces of dozens of strange helmets: caps covered in rows of boar's tusks.

Boar's-tusk helmets are known from more than 50 finds, mostly from graves, on the Greek mainland, the islands and Crete. They run from about 1850 BC to about 1100 BC, so "more than three thousand years old" holds for all of them. What survives is mostly the tusk plates: nothing is left of the cap itself, so the cap in the video is a reconstruction, partly from the Iliad's description.

  • Zarka, M. (2026). The manufacture of the Aegean boar's tusk helmets. prehistoire.org/offres/doc_inline_src/515/SPF%2B-%2BWBRG-%2B28%2BZarka%2BV003%2BBAT%2BBD.pdf
  • National Archaeological Museum, Athens: boar's tusk helmet NAMA 6568 (Mycenae, tomb 515), via World History Encyclopedia. worldhistory.org/image/2179/mycenaean-boars-tusk-helmet
  • Wikipedia. Boar's tusk helmet. en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boar%27s_tusk_helmet
0:22
One took the tusks of at least forty boars. For one hat.

A helmet from Kolonna on Aegina (about 1850-1700 BC) had about 80 plates from 40 or more boars, and large helmets needed 150 or more plates, about 75 boars. "At least forty" is the safe end of that range.

  • Zarka, M. (2026). The manufacture of the Aegean boar's tusk helmets. prehistoire.org/offres/doc_inline_src/515/SPF%2B-%2BWBRG-%2B28%2BZarka%2BV003%2BBAT%2BBD.pdf
  • Wikipedia. Boar's tusk helmet. en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boar%27s_tusk_helmet
0:26
And in the Iliad, Homer puts exactly this kind of helmet on Odysseus's head

It is the Iliad, not the Odyssey: Book 10, the night raid, where Meriones lends Odysseus a boar's-tusk helmet. It is the only such helmet in either epic.

  • Homer, Iliad 10.260-271 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb; Perseus Digital Library). cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0012/tlg001/perseus-eng3/10.218-11.84
0:31
centuries after anyone had stopped wearing one

The poems took shape around 750-650 BC (one scholar, M. L. West, dates the Odyssey to about 630 BC), while the latest helmets in graves date to about 1150-1100 BC. That gap is the "centuries".

  • Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2018.06.39 (on M. L. West's dates for the poems). bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=31848
  • Encyclopaedia Britannica. Homeric epics. britannica.com/art/Homeric-epics
0:40
this poem is carrying something more real than any monster: the memory of a world that vanished

This is the video's own reading, not an outside fact. The poems mix Bronze Age relics (the boar's-tusk helmet, Ajax's tower shield) with later Iron Age details (iron, Phoenician traders, cremation), so they carry memory of a lost world rather than a record of events. "More real than any monster" is our judgement.

  • Sherratt, S. (1990). Reading the texts. Antiquity. cambridge.org/core/product/897FDBD8C0DFC940CA42E4699E2841E4
  • Homer, Odyssey 9.528-535 and the other passages discussed in the chapters below (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1

0:56Meet Odysseus

0:56
King of Ithaca, a small, rocky island in western Greece.

Odysseus himself calls Ithaca rugged (9.27). Which modern island is Homer's Ithaca is debated (the "Paliki" theory of 2005, for one), so "Ithaca" here means the traditional island.

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.21-27 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
1:00
Famous for being clever, for lying whenever it helps, and for coming home very, very late.

The poem's first line calls him polytropos, "man of many ways" (1.1). Athena teases him about his love of tricks (13.291-299), and Homer says "he made the many lies he told seem like truth" (19.203). The poem is about his late homecoming (1.1-4).

  • Homer, Odyssey 1.1-4, 13.291-299 and 19.203 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136
1:10
ask the evidence for a verdict. Real. Myth. Or, annoyingly often, sort of.

The three stamps are the video's own verdicts, not an outside rating. Each rests on the sources in the chapter where it appears.

  • The video's own reading, not an outside source; the evidence behind each part is in the chapters of this page.

1:20First Stop: Troy

1:20
In 1870, a businessman named Heinrich Schliemann started digging into a hill in north-west Turkey.

Schliemann's first trenches at the hill of Hisarlik in 1870 had no permit. The official dig started in 1871.

  • Easton, D. (2014). The First Excavations at Troy. Studia Troica Monographien 5. publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/73442
1:26
Another man, Frank Calvert, had dug there first. Schliemann got the fame.

Calvert owned part of the hill and dug there in 1863-65. John Brunton had dug there even earlier, in 1856.

  • Schliemann-Museum Ankershagen. Frank Calvert. schliemann-museum.de/raum-10/calvert
  • Easton, D. (2014). The First Excavations at Troy. Studia Troica Monographien 5. publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/73442
1:32
Inside the hill were about nine cities, stacked one on top of the other

The hill holds nine main layers, Troy I to Troy IX. Excavators have counted far more phases (Carl Blegen about 46), so "about nine" is the headline count.

  • Nylander, C. (1963). The Fall of Troy. Antiquity. cambridge.org/core/product/38C14818C7610635984F1AA03EFC0BA9
  • Easton, D. (2014). The First Excavations at Troy. Studia Troica Monographien 5. publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/73442
1:40
One of those cities burned around 1200 BC. In its streets, archaeologists found human bones next to piles of sling stones.

The layer is called Troy VIIa. It burned between about 1210 and 1190 BC (Mountjoy) or about 1180 BC (Korfmann); Blegen's older date of about 1250 BC is out of date. It is the only destruction at Troy "definitely attributed to hostile action".

  • Hope Simpson, R. Mycenaean Greece and Homeric Tradition, ch. 2 (eCampusOntario Pressbooks). ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/mycenaeangreeceandhomerictradition/chapter/troy-and-the-trojan-war-archaeology-history-and-the-epic-traditions
  • Korfmann, M. (2004). Was There a Trojan War? Archaeology. archaeology.org/issues/online/features/was-there-a-trojan-war
1:49
Someone attacked it. Nobody knows who. The Greeks, after a ten-year war over a stolen queen?

Who attacked is unknown: raiders or the "Sea Peoples" are possible, and a Greek coalition is unlikely because the Greek palaces were already collapsing. The ten years and the stolen queen come from the poem's tradition. Hittite tablets show a place called Wilusa and the Ahhiyawa (the Greeks) in contact, but no text records a Greek siege of Troy.

  • Hope Simpson, R. Mycenaean Greece and Homeric Tradition, ch. 2 (eCampusOntario Pressbooks). ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/mycenaeangreeceandhomerictradition/chapter/troy-and-the-trojan-war-archaeology-history-and-the-epic-traditions
  • Beckman, G., Bryce, T. and Cline, E. (2011). The Ahhiyawa Texts. Society of Biblical Literature. sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Beckman_Ahhiyawa.pdf
1:55
The city is real, the fire is real, and the timing is right. Not proof. But not nothing.

"The timing is right" means the burned layer's date fits the period the war tradition points to. That is a fit, not proof, and the war reading is still contested. The bones are shown respectfully, with no gore.

  • Hope Simpson, R. Mycenaean Greece and Homeric Tradition, ch. 2 (eCampusOntario Pressbooks). ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/mycenaeangreeceandhomerictradition/chapter/troy-and-the-trojan-war-archaeology-history-and-the-epic-traditions
2:03
Odysseus sets off for home with twelve ships: down the Aegean, around the southern tip of Greece, and up to Ithaca.

Twelve ships is the poem's number (Odyssey 9.159-160), and the Catalogue of Ships in the Iliad gives him twelve too (2.631-637). To keep the video short we skip his first stop, a raid on the Ciconians in Thrace (9.39-66).

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.159-160 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
  • Homer, Iliad 2.631-637 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb; Perseus Digital Library).
2:10
But at that tip, Cape Malea, the north wind drives the fleet right off the map.

In the poem, waves, current and the North Wind carry him past Cythera (9.80-81).

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.80-84 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
2:16
Greek sailors had a saying about that cape: 'When you round Malea, forget your home.'

The saying is recorded by the ancient geographer Strabo: "But when you double Maleae, forget your home." The video gives it in a shorter modern wording.

  • Strabo, Geography 8.6.20 (tr. H. L. Jones, Loeb, 1917), LacusCurtius. penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/8F*.html

2:27Ten Years of Bad Decisions

2:27
they reach a land where a sweet fruit makes men forget their way home

The nine days of storm are in 9.82-83. The poem says those who eat the lotus lose the wish to return (9.94-97). Botanists read the lotus as the wild jujube (Ziziphus lotus), and no known fruit makes you forget, so that part is poetry.

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
2:31
an island of one-eyed giants, and a cave full of cheese

Odysseus's men want to take the cheese and leave; he wants to meet the owner (9.216-243).

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.106-115 and 9.216-243 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
2:39
The owner, the Cyclops Polyphemus, seals the cave with a boulder and starts eating them, two at a time.

Polyphemus is named at 1.70-71, and the boulder and the meals are in Book 9 (9.287-298).

  • Homer, Odyssey 1.68-71 and 9.287-298 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
2:50
So Odysseus gets him drunk, tells him his name is 'Nobody', and drives a burning stake into his eye.

"Nobody" is the Greek Outis (9.364-367). The blinding follows in the same book (9.375-394).

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.345-414 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
3:06
At dawn, the men escape clinging under the bellies of his sheep.

The escape is in 9.424-461.

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.424-461 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
3:15
Sicily and Crete were once home to dwarf elephants. Their skulls have one huge hole in the middle, where the trunk attached.

Dwarf elephants really existed on Mediterranean islands. Palaeoloxodon falconeri lived only on Sicily and Malta and stood about 0.8-1 m at the shoulder; Crete had other dwarf species. The skull's large central opening is where the trunk attached.

  • Wikipedia. Palaeoloxodon falconeri. en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeoloxodon_falconeri
  • Fortey, R. (2000). They Might Be Giants (review of A. Mayor, The First Fossil Hunters). London Review of Books. lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v22/n21/richard-fortey/they-might-be-giants
3:27
A paleontologist suggested exactly that in 1914. Tempting idea. Nobody can prove it.

The suggestion is Othenio Abel's, from 1914. Whether the skulls inspired the myth is disputed: Adrienne Mayor finds it plausible but rejects Abel's claim that Empedocles saw the skulls, and a 2024 review lists "fossil elephants and cyclopes" among geomyths with "no evidential basis". The Greeks would have found fossils, not live animals, and Homer never puts Polyphemus in Sicily (that comes from later tradition).

  • Mayor, A. (2000). The First Fossil Hunters. Princeton University Press; reviewed by R. Fortey, London Review of Books. lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v22/n21/richard-fortey/they-might-be-giants
  • Witton, M. P. and Hing, R. (2024). Interdisciplinary Science Reviews (University of Portsmouth news item on the study). port.ac.uk/news-events-and-blogs/news/new-study-finds-dinosaur-fossils-did-not-inspire-the-mythological-griffin
  • Bradshaw et al. (2024). Proceedings of the Royal Society B. doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2024.0967
3:38
he does the dumbest thing in the whole poem: he shouts his real name.

Shouting his name is in the poem (9.491-505), and his crew begs him not to. "The dumbest thing in the whole poem" is our joke.

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.491-505 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
3:43
So the Cyclops prays to his father, Poseidon, god of the sea: let Odysseus never get home. Or if he must, let him come late, having lost all his men, on someone else's ship, to find trouble in his house.

Polyphemus's prayer is at 9.528-535. The video turns it into a four-item checklist (late, all men lost, someone else's ship, trouble at home), and the poem delivers each one: 12.403-425, 13.70-125, 16.245-253 and 16.205-206.

  • Homer, Odyssey 9.528-535 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=9:card=1
  • Homer, Odyssey 1.68-71 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136
3:59
Keep that list: the rest of this story is that curse coming true.

This framing is the video's own reading. The prophet Tiresias gives a similar forecast (11.100-137) without calling it Poseidon's curse.

4:04
Next, Aeolus, a king Zeus made keeper of the winds, gives Odysseus a bag with every bad wind tied up inside.

Aeolus rules a floating island ringed by a wall of bronze (10.1-4), and the son of Cronos (Zeus) "had made him keeper of the winds" (10.21-22). The winds are tied in an ox-hide bag, with only the West Wind left free (10.19-27). "King" is our gloss: Homer calls Aeolus dear to the immortal gods and master of his island and household, but never uses the word.

  • Homer, Odyssey 10.1-55 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=10:card=1
4:12
On the tenth day, Ithaca is in sight. Then Odysseus falls asleep, his crew opens the bag looking for gold, and the winds blow them all the way back.

On the tenth day they see Ithaca (10.28-30). The crew suspect gold and silver in the bag (10.34-36).

  • Homer, Odyssey 10.28-55 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=10:card=1
4:23
Soon after, a tribe of giant cannibals sinks eleven of his twelve ships.

These are the Laestrygonians, giants who destroy eleven ships; only Odysseus's own ship, moored outside the harbor, escapes (10.121-132).

  • Homer, Odyssey 10.80-143 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=10:card=1
4:32
She serves his men a meal laced with drugs, to make them forget their homes.

Circe's drug makes them "utterly forget their native land" (10.235-236), and the men turned into pigs keep their human minds (10.240).

  • Homer, Odyssey 10.233-240 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D10%3Acard%3D229
4:40
Then one tap of her wand turns them into pigs.

In the poem the drug causes the forgetting and the wand does the transformation (10.233-240).

  • Homer, Odyssey 10.233-240 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D10%3Acard%3D229
4:44
On his way to save them, Odysseus meets the god Hermes, who hands him an antidote: moly, black at the root, with a flower as white as milk.

Moly is "black at the root, its flower like milk" (10.304-305). "Antidote" is our gloss for the herb Hermes gives him (10.277-324).

  • Homer, Odyssey 10.277-324 (tr. A. T. Murray, Loeb, 1919; Perseus Digital Library). perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0136:book=10:card=1
4:55
In 1983, two neurologists suggested that Circe's drug works a lot like the nightshade poisons, which scramble memory.

The neurologists are Andreas Plaitakis and Roger Duvoisin, and they framed it as a "may". Their paper's title names stramonium (Datura), a plant generally treated as coming from the New World, so the video says "nightshade poisons" and draws a henbane-like nightshade, never Datura.

5:03
And moly might be the snowdrop, which contains galantamine, a compound that counters them.

A 2024 reappraisal calls the snowdrop match "at the very least, questionable"; moly may be a group of plants rather than one. Snowdrops are toxic: nothing in the video suggests eating a plant.

Today, galantamine is used to ease the symptoms of Alzheimer's.

Galantamine (sold as Reminyl) was approved by the US FDA on 28 February 2001; Sweden approved it in March 2000. It eases symptoms of mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease. It is not a cure.

5:39
Their song promises knowledge: everything that happened at Troy. Around them lie the bones of sailors who stopped to listen.

The Sirens promise to tell "all that happens on the fruitful earth", Troy included (12.184-191); the bones are at 12.45-46. On screen they are birds with women's heads: Homer gives them no body, so the video takes the look from early Greek art, where by the 7th century BC sirens were regularly shown as human-headed birds. Fish-tailed sirens are medieval.

And the song is about him: "Come here, famous Odysseus. We know everything you suffered at Troy." He begs his men to untie him. They can't hear a word, so they tie him tighter and row until the song is gone.

In Homer the Sirens call him by name ("Come hither, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans") and say they know all that the Greeks and the Trojans suffered at Troy (12.184-191). "As far as we know" matters: the gap is an argument from silence. The chain (Iliad 10.266-271): Autolycus stole the helmet from Amyntor at Eleon, then it passed to Amphidamas of Cythera, then to Molus as a guest-gift, then to his son Meriones, then to Odysseus. Autolycus is Odysseus's mother's father (Odyssey 19.394-412).

13:10Real or Myth?

13:10
Centuries later, the Greeks borrowed an alphabet from Phoenician traders, and someone finally wrote the poem down.

The Greeks borrowed the Phoenician alphabet in the 8th century BC and added vowels; the date is debated. "From traders" is shorthand. The poem was written down sometime between about 700 and 520 BC, depending on the scholar; the later story of an "edition of Peisistratus" is not claimed here.

13:17
Then scribes copied it by hand for two thousand years.

The oldest papyrus scraps are from Egypt (3rd century BC), the Alexandrian edition is from about 150 BC (Aristarchus), the oldest near-complete copy is a Byzantine manuscript of about the 10th century now in Florence, and the first printed Homer appeared in Florence in 1488. From about 520 BC to 1488 is about 2,000 years of hand copying.

13:20
Odysseus took ten years to get home. His story took about three thousand to reach you.

"About three thousand" is a round figure. Counted from when the poems took shape (about 750-650 BC) it is about 2,700 years; counted from the events they remember (about 1200 BC) it is about 3,200. The ten years are the voyage home (the twentieth year is ten of war plus ten of voyage).

The places: often real.

"Often", not "always": Troy VIIa, Pylos and the Strait of Messina are backed by the sources above. Ancient identifications of other stops (the Cyclops by Etna, Aeolus in the Lipari islands, Circe at Monte Circeo) date from after Homer and are traditions, not proof.
